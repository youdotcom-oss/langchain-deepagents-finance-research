## 2. Structural Drivers of Growth

Malta's 2025 growth is best characterised as a **services exports + labour-force expansion** model rather than a capital-deepening story. The expenditure decomposition confirms this: consumption, government spending, and net exports account for essentially all of the +4.0% real growth, while GFCF was flat (−0.01pp). On the production side, the three largest contributors — Trade/Transport/Hotels (+1.49pp), ICT (+0.81pp), and Public Services (+0.78pp) — together account for roughly 3.1 percentage points of growth [4][5][7][8][9][10][13][16].

### Tourism and the Visitor Economy
Tourism is the **single largest directly observable driver** in 2025. The Trade/Transport/Hotels (G–I) sector grew +8.8%, contributing +1.49pp — roughly one-third of total GDP growth. Inbound tourism expenditure accounts for an estimated mid-to-high 20s percent of GDP, making Malta one of the most tourism-intensive economies in the EU. Pre-COVID, Malta received more than **5 tourists per resident per year** [6]. MTA data confirm continued strength of visitor numbers in 2024–2025 [7].

### iGaming and ICT Digital Services
ICT sector growth (+6.9%, +0.81pp) encompasses iGaming, fintech, payments and professional technology services — all part of Malta's deliberate strategy to attract digital-economy businesses via the Malta Gaming Authority (MGA) licensing framework. The gaming sector alone accounts for roughly a **high-single-digit share of GVA directly** and approximately **~12% of GVA including indirect/induced effects** [2][8][9][13]. Direct employment is roughly **~10,000 jobs**, with ~15,000 including broader supporting activity (~3–5% of total employment) [8][9][13]. The sector is high value-added relative to its employment share — making it a disproportionate contributor to GDP and the tax base.

### Financial and Professional Services
Financial services (funds, insurance, payments, fintech) are strategically important as exports and contribute to Malta's services trade surplus, but they appear to have been a **smaller marginal driver in 2025** than tourism or ICT — likely contributing tenths of a percentage point rather than full percentage points [14][15]. FinanceMalta and FT coverage frame the sector as central to Malta's long-term strategy but not the primary 2025 growth impulse.

### Migration-Driven Labour Force Expansion
Malta's population and labour force have grown rapidly through inward migration (both EU and non-EU). The labour force rose from approximately **280,600 in 2021** to **317,400 in 2024** and ~**319,700 in 2025** [10][16]. This labour supply expansion is a critical **growth enabler**: it allowed Malta's services, tourism, healthcare and logistics sectors to keep scaling output without hitting wage-driven inflationary ceilings. The unemployment rate of ~3.0% in 2025 reflects sustained labour demand. Importantly, migration raises **total GDP** faster than **GDP per capita** — headline growth flatters living-standard improvements [5][12].

## 3. Labour Market Dynamics and Migration

### Tight Labour Market
Malta's labour market remained exceptionally tight in 2024–2025, with unemployment near record lows (~3.0%) and full-time employment still rising year-on-year [10][16]. IMF Article IV 2025 highlights Malta's low unemployment and strong labour demand [5][12].

### Effects of Inward Migration
Net inward migration has produced four main macroeconomic effects:

1. **Expanded labour supply** — filled critical shortages in tourism, construction, gaming, healthcare, logistics and administration that the domestic workforce could not satisfy alone.
2. **Contained wage growth** — foreign labour supply moderated nominal wage inflation in lower- and middle-wage service sectors, even in a tight market. Wages rose but not explosively.
3. **Boosted aggregate GDP more than GDP per capita** — because both output and population grew rapidly, per-capita income gains lagged headline GDP growth significantly.
4. **Created capacity and infrastructure pressure** — IMF and domestic policy discussions point to housing, transport, public services and environmental stress [5][12].

## 4. iGaming Sector: Regulatory Environment and Risks

### Regulatory Evolution
The MGA has evolved from a "licensing hub" model toward intensive, risk-based supervision. Recent framework emphasis includes: risk-based licensing; AML/CFT controls; fit-and-proper checks; player protection/responsible gambling; licence cancellations and enforcement actions; and cooperation with the FIAU [2][8][9]. Malta's regulatory credibility — especially post-FATF greylisting and subsequent removal — is now a core competitive asset alongside tax and EU market-access advantages.

### Key Risks to iGaming
1. **EU market fragmentation** — major European countries (Germany, Sweden, Netherlands) have moved toward national licensing and local taxation, reducing the value of cross-border Malta-based licences.
2. **AML/reputational risk** — any renewed FATF/MONEYVAL concerns would damage gaming, finance and payments activity simultaneously.
3. **Tax and compliance pressure** — global minimum tax, rising compliance costs and national gambling levies could compress operator margins.
4. **Jurisdictional competition** — Gibraltar, Isle of Man, Curaçao and others compete for operators; large gaming groups can relocate functions.
5. **Social/political risk** — problem gambling regulation, advertising restrictions and consumer-protection litigation are persistent risk vectors [2][8][9][13].

## 5. Tourism Concentration Risk

### Scale of Exposure
A narrow Eurostat measure (accommodation and food services) puts tourism at roughly **high-single-digit GVA** and ~**one-tenth of employment**. A broader tourism-satellite-account view raises this to the **low-to-mid teens of GDP and employment**, with indirect/induced effects higher still [1][6].

### COVID Stress Test
The COVID shock is the most informative stress test available:
- Tourist arrivals collapsed from **>5 tourists per resident** pre-COVID to ~**1.3 per resident in 2020** [6].
- IMF described Malta as severely affected by the tourism stop; real GDP contracted sharply in 2020 [8][9].
- The rebound was fast once borders reopened, confirming both dependence and resilience — but the 2020 experience shows that without iGaming and financial services as buffers, the shock would have been larger.

**Assessment:** Malta is **highly vulnerable to sudden external shocks** (pandemic restrictions, airline disruption, European consumer downturns, geopolitical travel disruption). Diversification into gaming, finance and professional services cushions but does not eliminate this risk.

## 6. Housing Market and Cost of Living

Malta's housing market remained tight in 2024–2025, with house prices rising at **mid-single-digit rates** in nominal terms and rents under stronger pressure, particularly in areas with high foreign-worker, student, tourism and short-let demand [2][3][5].

Affordability stress is real and structural:
- Rapid population growth (migration-driven) has lifted housing demand faster than the stock and infrastructure can adjust.
- Most new workers rent rather than buy, so **rent pressure is more acute** than mortgage stress.
- IMF commentary has repeatedly flagged housing affordability, infrastructure strain and labour-market dependence on foreign workers as macro-social constraints [3][5].
- FT coverage highlighted Malta's construction boom, which expanded supply but entrenched a growth model tied to property development, land use and migration demand [12].

**Assessment:** Housing affordability stress is a genuine structural challenge linked to Malta's migration-dependent growth model, not merely a credit-cycle phenomenon.

## 7. Sustainability of Public Spending Growth (+1.00pp G Contribution)

### Fiscal Position
Malta's general government deficit is approximately **3–3.5% of GDP** in 2024–2025, and public debt is roughly **47–50% of GDP** — below the euro-area average and below the Maastricht 60% threshold, but materially above pre-COVID levels [3][7][11].

### Concern: Structure of the 2025 Growth Mix
With government contributing ~**25% of total 2025 GDP growth** (+1.00pp of +4.00pp), while private investment (GFCF) was flat (−0.01pp), the growth composition raises sustainability questions:
- The deficit is at or near the EU 3% reference level.
- Energy and cost-of-living subsidies have been broad rather than targeted; recurrent public wages/social spending are hard to unwind.
- Flat GFCF signals that public demand is **not crowding in** private investment.
- Some Malta-specific fiscal channels (e.g., golden passport programme) face legal and political vulnerability [10].

IMF and EU recommendations call for gradual consolidation, better-targeted subsidies, and reorienting public spending toward productivity-enhancing infrastructure [3][5][7][11].

**Assessment:** Malta has adequate fiscal space in the short run, but the 2025 growth mix (high G, flat I) is not the ideal composition for durable medium-term growth.

## 8. Growth Deceleration: Normalization or Structural Concern?

### Decomposing the Deceleration
| Phase | Driver |
|---|---|
| 2021 (+13.4%) | Post-lockdown reopening; tourism catch-up; base effects from 2020 contraction |
| 2022 (+2.6%) | Energy price shock; post-rebound slowdown |
| 2023 (+10.6%) | Full tourism normalisation; residual catch-up; digital services expansion |
| 2024 (+6.2%) | Continued services growth; some moderation |
| 2025 (+4.0%) | Normalisation; emerging capacity constraints |

The deceleration from double-digit reopening growth to +4.0% is **primarily a healthy normalization**. A +4.0% rate is still roughly **twice the euro-area average** and IMF/EC material characterises Malta's growth as robust relative to EU peers [3][5][11].

### Structural Constraints Now Visible
However, the slowdown also exposes real capacity limits that will make sustaining even 4% growth more demanding:
- **Labour shortages** — growth relies heavily on imported labour; domestic supply is exhausted.
- **Housing and infrastructure bottlenecks** — transport, utilities, schools, health and waste capacity face pressure from population and tourism intensity.
- **Tourism carrying capacity** — increasing visitor numbers strain limited land and services.
- **Weak investment signal** — flat GFCF in 2025 suggests firms are not expanding productive capacity despite strong demand.
- **Productivity risk** — growth driven by more labour, tourism headcount and public demand is less durable than capital-deepening or productivity-led growth [3][5][11].

The current-account deficit of −0.8% of GDP is small and not an immediate external vulnerability signal. The bigger risks are domestic capacity: housing, labour, infrastructure and environmental limits.

## 9. Structural vs. Cyclical Assessment

| Dimension | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Primary growth drivers** | STRUCTURAL — tourism, iGaming, financial services, migration are long-standing model elements |
| **2025 growth level (+4.0%)** | CYCLICAL normalisation from post-pandemic reopening peaks |
| **Tourism concentration risk** | STRUCTURAL vulnerability — COVID confirms deep exposure |
| **Housing/affordability stress** | STRUCTURAL — migration-demand-driven, not credit-cycle |
| **Public spending contribution** | CYCLICAL — elevated G contribution likely reflects cost-of-living support; not permanently sustainable at current scale |
| **Flat GFCF** | CYCLICAL/CONCERN — needs monitoring; if investment stays flat into 2026–27, this becomes a structural drag |
| **iGaming regulatory risk** | STRUCTURAL — EU market fragmentation and AML risks are long-run headwinds |
| **Labour force expansion** | STRUCTURAL driver; also a STRUCTURAL constraint (infrastructure capacity) |

**Overall verdict:** Malta's +4.0% 2025 growth is a **healthy normalization** from post-pandemic reopening highs, not a warning signal. Malta's structural growth model — services exports (tourism + digital/iGaming + finance) amplified by migration-driven labour supply — remains intact and explains its persistent above-EU-average performance. However, concentration in tourism and iGaming, flat private investment, housing affordability stress, and infrastructure bottlenecks represent genuine structural risks. Malta's medium-term growth potential (likely 3–4%) is solid but increasingly constrained by capacity rather than demand.
