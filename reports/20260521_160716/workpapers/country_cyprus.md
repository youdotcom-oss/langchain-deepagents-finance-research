## 2. What Drives Sustained ~3.8–3.9% Growth?

### 2.1 Services-Led, Export-Driven Expansion

Cyprus's growth is primarily **services-export led rather than a domestic credit or construction boom**. The expenditure decomposition confirms this: net exports contributed the single largest component at **+2.45pp**, while private consumption added +1.90pp [8][11][16]. The IMF and European Commission describe Cyprus as a "nimble, dynamic" economy with resilience built on an expanding high-value services base [8][11][16].

### 2.2 Tourism

Tourism is one of the clearest cyclical supports. By 2024–2025, Cyprus had fully normalised from the pandemic shock and services exports — including tourism receipts — were above pre-Covid levels. Pre-pandemic benchmarks established approximately **4.0 million international tourist arrivals in 2019** [4]. The Trade/transport/accommodation sector (NACE G-I) growing at **+6.4% in 2025**, contributing +1.31pp to GDP, is consistent with tourism at or above pre-pandemic highs and continued gains in higher-spending visitor segments.

### 2.3 ICT and Business Services Expansion

The ICT sector (NACE J) expanded at **+8.0%** in 2025, contributing +0.83pp to GDP growth. Cyprus growth is increasingly supported by higher-value services (technology, gaming, fintech, professional services) rather than only traditional tourism [15][16]. The tech/ICT expansion reflects the dual impact of relocated international firms (especially post-2022) and an indigenous ecosystem, creating a structural shift in the economy's composition.

### 2.4 Financial and Professional Services

Services account for a dominant share of Cyprus value added [3][5]. Commercial service exports roughly doubled versus 2019 levels in World Bank structured data, with finance/insurance still a meaningful component [3][5]. While the offshore banking model has been substantially dismantled post-2013, Cyprus retains a significant role in financial intermediation, fund management, holding-company structures, and business services.

### 2.5 EU/RRF Investment and Reform Momentum

EU Recovery and Resilience Facility disbursements and ongoing reform implementation provided a background fiscal and investment support. While GFCF contributed 0.00pp in 2025, this may reflect timing of RRF disbursements rather than absence of public investment.

## 3. Why Is Inflation So Low (0.8% HICP) Despite Solid Growth?

### 3.1 Energy Disinflation

The most significant explanation is lower or stabilised energy prices. Cyprus inflation is highly exposed to imported energy and electricity costs. European Commission material explicitly links Cyprus's disinflation path to lower energy-price pressures, with HICP inflation expected to remain low relative to euro-area average [15]. Official Cyprus HICP releases confirm very subdued monthly/annual inflation in 2025 [12][13].

### 3.2 Growth Without Domestic Overheating

The critical structural insight is that ~3.8% GDP growth did **not** require broad domestic overheating because a large part came from net services exports. With NX contributing +2.45pp — the single largest expenditure component — much of Cyprus's growth was essentially external demand flowing through resident service providers. This does not generate the same inflationary pressure as equivalent domestically-demand-led growth [15][16].

### 3.3 Small Open Economy Pass-Through

Cyprus imports a large share of tradable goods. When global goods and energy inflation falls, headline HICP can fall quickly through import-price pass-through. Competition in retail and tourism-related services, plus base effects from the 2022–23 energy shock, also dampened inflation [12][13].

### 3.4 Housing/Wages Caveat

Housing costs — particularly rents in Limassol and Nicosia — have risen meaningfully due to foreign professionals, relocated businesses, and tourism-related demand. However, HICP housing weights and measurement differ from market rent/real-estate anecdotes, and this pressure may not fully translate into headline HICP. Wage data were not available in retrieved sources to confirm the absence of a wage-price spiral, but the inflation outcome suggests any wage gains were either moderate, productivity-supported, or offset by lower energy/import costs.

## 4. Current Account Deficit of −6.4% GDP: Sustainability Assessment

### 4.1 Scale and Context

A deficit of **−6.4% of GDP** is large and warrants monitoring. IMF annual series suggested the deficit may have been closer to **−8% of GDP** for 2024–25 in some vintage estimates, making the magnitude potentially even larger [2][16]. Either way, it is material by EU standards.

### 4.2 Structural Composition

Cyprus's external balance has an unusual structure:

- **Goods balance: deeply negative.** Cyprus imports energy, food, consumer goods, machinery, transport equipment, and construction inputs. This is the main structural drag.
- **Services balance: strongly positive.** Tourism, transport/shipping, financial services, ICT, and business services offset a large part of the goods deficit [3][5]. The dominance of NX (+2.45pp expenditure contribution) reflects this services surplus.
- **Primary income: often negative.** Foreign-owned companies, investment vehicles, banks, funds, and multinationals repatriate profits and dividends. This keeps the current account in deficit even when services exports are strong.
- **Special-purpose entity distortion.** Cyprus external accounts can be distorted by SPEs, shipping, aircraft/leasing, and multinational balance-sheet flows, making headline ratios require careful interpretation.

### 4.3 IMF/EC Stance

IMF and European Commission material treats Cyprus's external position as a monitoring issue rather than an acute crisis risk [11][15][16]. The economy's large services-export base and continued FDI inflows provide financing. The key policy concern is whether the deficit is financed by stable productive FDI or volatile short-term flows. The IMF's 2025 Article IV flagged external shocks, energy exposure, and real-estate concentration as the main risks [7].

### 4.4 Verdict

The deficit is **not automatically unsustainable** given the large services-export base and continued foreign capital attraction. But it is **not benign**: a persistent deficit above 5% of GDP leaves Cyprus exposed to tourism shocks, energy-price reversals, and shifts in foreign financing conditions. The current-account position is a **structural vulnerability**, not an imminent crisis.

## 5. Role of Russian/Ukrainian Diaspora Relocations Post-2022

### 5.1 Significance of the 2022 Relocation Wave

Cyprus received a **large, economically visible inflow of Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian tech workers, founders, and "foreign-interest" companies** after February 2022. However, official GDP and labour statistics do not isolate "Russian/Ukrainian relocations" as a separate line item [14]. The effects are measurable indirectly through ICT GVA, high-skilled employment, office and housing demand, rental inflation, and services exports.

Bloomberg reported early in the war that the IMF viewed Cyprus as vulnerable to Ukraine-war spillovers because of prior ties through tourism, professional services, company structures, and deposits [14]. Instead of simply losing these links, Cyprus benefited simultaneously from a high-skilled relocation wave [8].

Cyprus was **already pre-positioned** for this wave: many Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian tech firms were already headquartered, incorporated, or partly based in Cyprus before February 2022. The war often **accelerated staff relocation, restructuring, or de-Russification** rather than creating entirely new Cyprus presences.

### 5.2 Key Companies and Subsectors

Notable entities in the Cyprus tech ecosystem with post-Soviet roots:

- **Wargaming** — Belarus-origin gaming group, long headquartered in Cyprus; exited Russia and Belarus after 2022. Gaming/publishing contributes directly to NACE J GVA.
- **GDEV / Nexters** — mobile gaming group with Cyprus domicile and Russian-founder roots.
- **Easybrain and other mobile gaming studios** — Cyprus as a hub for mobile-game development, publishing, monetisation, and headquarters.
- **Exness, FxPro, XM / Trading Point** — online trading/fintech groups employing large technology, payments, compliance and platform teams in Cyprus.
- **Amdocs, 3CX, and broader software/IT-services companies** — part of the wider software and IT-services base [2][5][8].

### 5.3 Has the Flow Continued or Reversed?

The relocation wave has **slowed and become more selective** rather than fully reversing:

- The emergency 2022–23 relocation wave moderated by 2024–25.
- EU, US, and UK sanctions concerns made Cypriot banks, lawyers, and accountants more cautious about Russian-linked clients.
- Cyprus has strengthened sanctions compliance and AML supervision to distance itself from the "Russian offshore" reputation.
- Some Russian-linked entrepreneurs and capital migrated to the UAE, Armenia, Serbia, or Kazakhstan.
- However, **non-sanctioned technology companies and skilled workers still find Cyprus attractive**: EU access, English-speaking professional services, favourable tax regime, quality schools, safety, and Middle East/Eastern Europe proximity.

The old Russian private-wealth/professional-services model has weakened; the **compliant tech-headquarters model has continued** [8].

## 6. ICT Sector Boom (+8.0% GVA in 2025)

### 6.1 Subsector Drivers

Cyprus's NACE J sector encompasses software publishing, video-game publishing, film/audio/media, broadcasting, telecoms, computer programming/consultancy, and data processing/hosting. The **+8.0% GVA growth in 2025** (+0.83pp GDP contribution) appears most consistent with strength in:

1. **Computer programming and IT consultancy** — software development, product engineering, cloud services, cybersecurity, enterprise platforms, and outsourced development. Likely the largest structural driver.
2. **Gaming and mobile-app publishing** — Cyprus hosts or services several international gaming groups; gaming generates high revenue per employee.
3. **Fintech and online trading technology** — engineering, platform, payments, data, and cybersecurity staff from groups like Exness, FxPro, and XM support the ICT labour market.
4. **Headquarters and management functions of foreign-interest tech firms** — contributing directly through wages, office spending, and local procurement.
5. **Indigenous start-up growth** — local accelerators, university-linked talent, and start-up activity, though likely smaller than the relocated/international firm contribution [2][5].

KPMG-cited industry reporting confirms Cyprus's rising tech sector is now a meaningful contributor to GDP and job creation [2].

### 6.2 Attribution

The +8.0% growth was probably driven by a **combination of relocated/international software and gaming firms, fintech platform activity, and local ecosystem growth**, with the first two likely the most important marginal drivers. Public national accounts do not split the ICT GVA increase between these sub-categories.

## 7. Real Estate and Construction Dynamics

### 7.1 Price Trends 2024–2025

Central Bank of Cyprus residential property price index data show prices continued rising through 2024 and into 2025, with **apartments rising faster than houses** [9][10][11][12][13]. This is consistent with:

- Strong demand for urban apartments from foreign professionals and relocating companies;
- Students and private-university expansion;
- Tourism-linked short-term rental demand;
- Limited new supply in prime locations;
- High construction costs;
- Permanent-residency investors and non-EU buyers.

**Limassol** has been the clearest pressure point (primary hub for tech/fintech firms), but Nicosia, Larnaca, and Paphos also experienced strong demand.

### 7.2 Construction Growth (+4.5%)

Construction growth of ~+4.5% in 2025 (+0.22pp GDP) is supported by:

- Housing demand from foreign professionals and families;
- Office space for technology, fintech, and professional-services firms;
- Tourism and hospitality projects;
- Local household demand;
- Infrastructure and public projects;
- Replacement of older stock with higher-density apartment projects.

Relocated businesses are an important demand factor but **not the exclusive driver**.

### 7.3 Government Schemes

Key distinction for the current period:

- The controversial **citizenship-by-investment programme was abolished in 2020** following EU and domestic political pressure. It is not a 2024–25 driver.
- Cyprus retains **permanent residency by investment** (typically linked to qualifying real estate investment), supporting housing demand.
- The **Business Facilitation Unit / foreign-interest company regime** enables companies to bring non-EU staff, supporting housing and office demand.
- The passport-for-property model is no longer officially available, moderating but not eliminating foreign investment as a real estate demand driver.

## 8. Financial Services Evolution Post-2013 Banking Crisis

### 8.1 The 2013 Structural Break

The 2013 crisis was a fundamental reset. Cyprus underwent: resolution of Laiki Bank; bail-in of uninsured Bank of Cyprus depositors; capital controls; sharp deleveraging; stricter bank supervision; pressure to reduce non-resident (especially Russian) deposits; NPL disposals and restructurings; and banking-system consolidation. ESM documentation records the 2013 rescue architecture and subsequent banking-sector restructuring [6].

### 8.2 Current Banking Sector State (2024–2025)

By 2024–25, the sector had substantially recovered:

- **Better capitalised**: banks hold materially higher capital ratios than pre-crisis.
- **Strong liquidity**: deposit bases rebuilt on a more diversified foundation.
- **Strong profitability (2022–2024)**: higher ECB rates boosted net interest margins, especially because Cypriot banks had large deposit bases with slow deposit repricing. Cyprus Mail noted in 2025 that bank profits were easing from very high levels as rate conditions normalised [15].
- **NPLs dramatically reduced**: from post-crisis peaks in the 30%+ range, NPL ratios fell to single digits — though still above the strongest euro-area banking systems. The IMF's 2025 Article IV described Cyprus's macro-financial position as resilient [7].
- **No longer oversized**: the sector is no longer the outsized offshore-banking system of the pre-2013 period.

### 8.3 Residual Risks

- Real-estate exposure (concentration risk given the housing boom);
- High private-sector debt legacy;
- Potential deterioration if rates, tourism, or external demand shock households or firms;
- Sanctions/AML reputational risk — Cyprus is under continued EU scrutiny;
- Concentration in a small number of large banks in a small economy.

## 9. Synthesis and Assessment

### 9.1 Causal Narrative

Cyprus's +3.8% growth in 2025 is the product of **four mutually reinforcing forces**:

1. **Tourism normalisation** — visitor volumes at or above pre-pandemic benchmarks, driving Trade/G-I sector +6.4% growth.
2. **Tech/ICT hub maturation** — accelerated by post-2022 relocation of Russian/Ukrainian/Belarusian tech firms and workers; gaming, software, and fintech infrastructure generating ICT J +8.0% growth.
3. **Resilient consumption** — supported by high-skilled immigrant workers, stable employment, and modest wage growth (+1.90pp from C).
4. **Strong net services exports** — the largest single expenditure driver at +2.45pp, reflecting competitive services base in tourism, financial services, and digital sectors.

The 0.8% HICP inflation reflects energy-price disinflation and the export-led nature of growth (no domestic overheating), while the −6.4% current account deficit reflects structural goods import dependency that the services surplus only partially offsets.

### 9.2 Structural vs. Cyclical Assessment

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| Tourism growth | **Cyclical** — tourism already normalised; further gains depend on external demand conditions |
| ICT/tech hub role | **STRUCTURAL** — company relocations and ecosystem investment are persistent; EU membership advantage durable |
| Inflation at 0.8% | **Partly cyclical** — energy disinflation will not persist indefinitely; housing cost pressures may eventually feed into HICP |
| Current account deficit | **STRUCTURAL** — chronic goods import dependency with structural services offset; unlikely to close materially |
| Financial sector resilience | **STRUCTURAL** — post-2013 deleveraging is permanent; NPL reduction durable absent new shock |
| Real estate boom | **Cyclical/structural mix** — foreign demand partly structural (relocated workers) but sensitive to geopolitical/policy changes |

**Overall verdict: STRUCTURAL with cyclical amplification.** The transition from an offshore-banking/tourism economy to a diversified services hub (tourism + ICT + professional services + financial services) represents a durable structural shift. The post-2022 tech relocation wave accelerated a structural upgrade that was already underway. Cyprus's above-EU-average growth is likely to persist as long as: (a) EU membership remains attractive to non-EU tech firms; (b) the AML/sanctions compliance posture holds; and (c) no major external shock (energy, geopolitical, tourism) reverses the services-export momentum.

## 10. Key Risks and Vulnerabilities

1. **Geopolitical shock to relocated tech community** — further EU sanctions restrictions or geopolitical escalation could reverse some post-2022 inflows.
2. **Current account financing** — persistent −6–8% of GDP deficit requires continued foreign capital attraction; a shift from productive FDI to volatile flows would increase vulnerability.
3. **Real estate correction risk** — housing prices elevated relative to local incomes; foreign-demand-driven segments are inherently volatile.
4. **Energy dependence** — Cyprus is an energy island with high import dependence; any energy price shock feeds directly into inflation and the current account.
5. **Banking concentration** — small number of large banks; real-estate exposure remains an NPL risk in a downturn.
6. **AML/reputational risk** — EU scrutiny of Cyprus financial system continues; reputational shock could affect financial services and company-headquarters attractiveness.
