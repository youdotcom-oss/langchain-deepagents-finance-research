## 2. Russia Trade Severance Impact

### Background
Finland historically maintained deep economic ties with Russia: energy imports (especially natural gas and electricity), transit logistics through Finnish ports, Russian tourist inflows (particularly to eastern Finland), and forest-products supply chains (Russian roundwood). The 2022 invasion of Ukraine and subsequent sanctions abruptly severed most of these ties.

### Quantified Impact Assessment
The Bank of Finland published early-2022 war scenarios quantifying the Ukraine/Russia shock as a meaningful drag on Finnish GDP growth and an inflation shock. However, the cumulative realized GDP loss attributable specifically to Russia trade severance through 2025 is difficult to isolate with precision [[10]].

Critically, a later Bank of Finland firm-level study concluded that the collapse of Russia trade had a **limited direct effect on Finnish manufacturing** overall, implying that the export-loss channel alone was not sufficient to explain Finland's post-2022 underperformance [[6]]. This suggests that while Russia decoupling lowered Finland's GDP *level* versus a no-war counterfactual, the 2024–2025 growth weakness is increasingly driven by domestic cyclical forces rather than pure Russia-trade arithmetic [[6], [13], [16]].

### Trade Reorientation
Broad trade reorientation has occurred for goods exporters, but at a cost [[6]]:
- **Energy:** Loss of cheap Russian gas and electricity imports raised input costs for energy-intensive industries (including forest products)
- **Wood supply:** Russian roundwood supplies, which Finnish mills depended on, were cut off, requiring sourcing from domestic or more distant supplies
- **Eastern logistics:** The Finnish border crossing and transit trade to Russia collapsed entirely
- **Russian tourism:** A significant source of demand for eastern Finnish border regions and the broader services sector
- **Net verdict:** IMF and Bank of Finland discussions confirm that post-2022 weakness shifted from Russia-specific to domestic-cyclical by 2024–2025. Russia decoupling created a *structural level shift* rather than an ongoing annual growth subtraction [[6], [13]].

## 3. Financial Sector GVA Collapse (−4.6%)

### Diagnosis: NIM/FISIM Compression, Not a Systemic Credit Event
The −4.6% financial sector GVA in 2025 is best explained by **net interest margin compression** as ECB rates were cut in 2024–2025, rather than a systemic loan-loss event.

### Mechanism
Finnish and Nordic banks (Nordea, OP Financial Group) benefited strongly from the 2022–2023 ECB rate-hiking cycle, which expanded net interest income (NII) significantly. As the ECB pivoted to rate cuts in mid-2024, this tailwind reversed:

- Reuters explicitly linked Nordea's 2025 results to **rate cuts weighing on interest income**, alongside a slow recovery in lending markets [[7], [11]]
- OP Financial Group's 2024 result remained strong, but 2025 reporting pointed to a **weaker rate-income environment** despite resilient overall profitability [[9], [12]]
- The Eurostat-measured GVA for financial services incorporates FISIM (Financial Intermediation Services Indirectly Measured), which is mechanically sensitive to interest-rate spreads and credit volumes. A narrowing of margins and weak loan demand can produce sharp GVA declines even when banks remain profitable.

### Credit Quality
Loan losses were a **secondary**, not primary, factor. The Bank of Finland highlighted risks from weak economy, housing/construction and real-estate exposures [[8]], but also assessed the Finnish banking sector as capable of withstanding even a harsher-than-forecast recession. This supports the interpretation that the −4.6% GVA fall reflects margin compression more than credit deterioration.

**Assessment:** CYCLICAL — driven by the rate cycle. Should reverse partially as lending volumes recover and margins stabilize, though the magnitude of the 2025 fall partly reflects the unsustainably high NII of 2022–2023 unwinding.

## 4. Forestry, Pulp, and Paper Sector (Industry B-E context)

### Structural Headwinds
Finland's forest-products sector (UPM-Kymmene, Stora Enso, Metsä Group) faced a convergence of structural headwinds in 2024–2025:

1. **Loss of Russian roundwood inputs** — Russian wood supply, historically a cost-competitive input for Finnish mills, was cut off after 2022. Substituting from domestic or other sources raised costs [[6]]
2. **Weak European paper demand** — Structural decline in graphic paper (newspapers, office paper) continued, while packaging demand was subdued amid weak European consumer spending
3. **High energy and labour costs** — Finnish industrial energy costs remain elevated relative to pre-2022 baselines
4. **Capacity rationalization** — Nordic forest-products companies have been restructuring capacity across their European mill networks

### Why Industry B-E Still Posted +1.5%
Despite forest-products stress, Industry B-E managed modest positive growth. This likely reflects:
- Partial offset from other manufacturing sub-sectors
- Chemical/pharmaceutical manufacturing
- Electronics/advanced manufacturing partially captured under B-E in Eurostat classification
- The +1.5% growth, yielding only +0.28pp GDP contribution, signals an industry sector that is growing but not dynamically

The Bank of Finland's Russia-trade firm-level analysis confirms that input-cost pressure from lost Russian wood and energy was real, even if aggregate manufacturing was not catastrophically impacted [[6]]. The available evidence supports a narrative of **structural adjustment and weak demand** rather than a single sharp shock.

**Assessment:** STRUCTURAL for paper/pulp (secular demand decline in print media + lost Russian inputs). Partially CYCLICAL for other manufacturing (linked to European demand recovery).

## 5. Household Consumption Weakness (−0.12pp despite 1.8% HICP)

### The Paradox
Finland's household consumption contributed negatively to GDP despite inflation running close to the ECB target. With real wages technically returning to positive territory, conventional theory would predict consumption recovery. The data contradict this.

### Explanations

**A) Variable-Rate Mortgage Overhang (primary factor)**
Finland has one of Europe's highest shares of variable-rate mortgages, historically ~90% Euribor-linked. The 2022–2023 ECB hiking cycle passed rapidly into Finnish household debt-service costs [[10]]:
- A household with a €200,000 mortgage facing +3pp rate increase would pay roughly **€6,000 more per year** in gross interest, crowding out other consumption
- Even as ECB rate cuts began in mid-2024, relief came with a lag — many households remained locked into elevated servicing costs through 2025 [[8], [13]]

**B) Weak Labour Market Confidence**
Finland's labour market weakened materially into 2024–2025. Positive real wage growth did not fully translate into spending, as unemployment fears prompted precautionary saving [[13], [16]].

**C) Housing Wealth Destruction**
Finnish housing prices fell sharply from their 2021–2022 peak and have only begun stabilizing. For households whose primary wealth is housing, this negative wealth effect suppressed confidence and spending, particularly on durable goods and home-improvement categories [[8], [13]].

**D) Debt Overhang and Precautionary Saving**
Consumer confidence indicators remained depressed through 2025, consistent with households rebuilding balance-sheet buffers rather than spending their modest real income gains. Bank of Finland's 2025 assessment described "new obstacles to recovery" and a cautious household sector [[13]].

**Assessment:** Primarily CYCLICAL — the mortgage rate relief from ECB cuts is working through with a lag. As housing prices stabilize and unemployment fears ease, consumption should recover. However, the structural variable-rate exposure creates persistent rate-sensitivity.

## 6. Nordic Divergence: Finland vs. Sweden (+1.5%) and Denmark (+2.9%)

### Comparative Performance Table
| Driver | Finland | Sweden | Denmark |
|--------|---------|--------|---------|
| 2025 real GDP | **+0.2%** | **+1.5%** | **+2.9%** |
| Housing rate sensitivity | Very high (90% variable) | High but recovering | Lower |
| Russia exposure | High (energy, wood, tourism, logistics) | Low | Negligible |
| Export engine | No dominant engine | Diversified recovery | Pharma/Novo Nordisk |
| Fiscal position | Deficit ~4% GDP | Lower debt/deficit | Surplus/low debt |
| ECB rate pass-through | Fastest in the Nordic region | Fast but recovering | Less rate-sensitive |

### Why Sweden Outperformed Finland (+1.3pp gap)
Sweden benefited from a broader recovery as inflation and rates fell, supporting household real income and domestic services activity. The European Commission's Sweden forecasts emphasize improving domestic demand as inflation and interest rates declined [[16]]. Sweden also lacked Finland's Russia-exposure legacy and deep construction recession, and its fiscal position offered more cushion.

### Why Denmark Dramatically Outperformed Finland (+2.7pp gap)
Denmark's exceptional 2025 GDP growth was heavily driven by the **pharma/Novo Nordisk effect** — a powerful pharmaceutical export engine that inflated Danish national accounts headline growth. Nordea analysis treats this pharma effect as central to Denmark's recent macro outperformance, while noting the non-pharma domestic economy was less exceptional [[15]]. Finland has no equivalent single-sector export engine.

### Structural vs. Cyclical Divergence
The Nordic divergence is **partly structural** (Denmark's pharma sector, Sweden's more diversified export base, Finland's Russia exposure) and **partly cyclical** (Finland's uniquely harsh mortgage pass-through, deeper construction recession). As the rate cycle normalizes, Finland should partially converge with Sweden, but the Denmark gap likely reflects durable structural differences in industrial composition.

## 7. Housing Market and Mortgage Rate Pass-Through

### Market Structure
Finland's mortgage market is structurally distinct from most of the eurozone:
- ~90% of Finnish housing loans historically variable-rate, tied to 12-month Euribor
- Repricing is rapid: most borrowers see rate changes within 12 months of an ECB policy move
- This makes Finland one of the most interest-rate-sensitive mortgage markets in the euro area [[10]]

### The 2022–2024 Rate Shock
- ECB rates rose from near-zero/negative in 2022 to restrictive levels by late 2023
- Euribor followed quickly, driving sharp increases in Finnish mortgage payments
- Bank of Finland assessed that most borrowers remained resilient but noted a subset of households pushed into financial stress [[6], [8]]

### ECB Cutting Cycle Relief (mid-2024 onward)
The ECB began cutting rates in mid-2024, and this is gradually reducing debt-service burdens for Finnish mortgage holders [[11]]. However:
- Relief is incomplete; many households still carrying 2023-peak rate exposure
- Unemployment and weak confidence still weigh on demand
- House prices remain below 2021–2022 peaks, stabilizing but not rebounding strongly
- Residential construction recovering from a very low base — new starts and permits remain well below boom-era levels [[9], [13]]

### Macro Effect on 2025 GDP
Even with GFCF contributing slightly positive (+0.17pp), the residential construction component remained a structural drag or minimal contributor. The housing investment cycle was not a growth engine in 2025.

## 8. Fiscal Position and EU Fund Absorption

### General Government Deficit
Finland is running a sizeable deficit, well above the EU's 3% threshold:
- **2024 deficit:** ~4.4% of GDP
- **2025 deficit:** ~4.0% of GDP
- Government debt on a rising path [[1], [2], [14]]

This contrasts sharply with Sweden and Denmark, both of which have much stronger public finance positions.

### Orpo Government Austerity (mid-2023 onward)
The National Coalition-led Orpo government implemented a broad consolidation package:
- **VAT increase:** Standard rate raised from 24% to **25.5%** in 2024 — a direct drag on household real purchasing power and consumption
- **Social benefit cuts:** Reductions in unemployment benefits, housing allowances, eligibility tightening
- **Index freezes:** Limiting benefit growth in real terms
- **Public sector restraint:** Pressure on health, social services and wellbeing-services authority (hyvinvointialue) spending
- **Labour market reforms:** Unemployment insurance changes, adjustments to collective bargaining rules [[2], [14]]

### Macro Trade-Off
Fiscal consolidation during a weak housing-led recovery has directly suppressed domestic demand. The −0.06pp Government consumption contribution reflects deliberate expenditure restraint, and the VAT hike contributed to weak household spending. The IMF supports fiscal consolidation to stabilize Finland's debt trajectory, but acknowledges the short-run macro trade-off [[2], [14]].

### EU RRF and Structural Funds
Finland's Recovery and Resilience Facility envelope is modest (~€2 billion total, spread over several years), focused on:
- Green transition investment
- Digitalization (relevant to ICT J sector's +5.1% performance)
- Employment reforms
- Health and social services

The RRF funds support GFCF slightly and may help explain why total investment did not collapse further, but the scale is insufficient to offset the housing construction slump [[12]]. EU funds are a **cushion, not a growth engine** in Finland's case.

## 9. Synthesis: Why Finland is at the Bottom of the EU-27

Finland's +0.2% GDP growth in 2025 reflects a **multi-factor stagnation** rather than any single shock:

| Factor | Nature | 2025 GDP Impact |
|--------|--------|----------------|
| Variable-rate mortgage pass-through | Structural exposure, cyclical manifestation | Large negative (consumption, construction) |
| Housing market recession (prices, construction) | Cyclical — turning but slowly | Negative (GFCF, consumption confidence) |
| Russia trade severance (energy, wood, tourism, logistics) | Structural level shift (front-loaded) | Ongoing low-level drag |
| Financial sector NIM compression | Cyclical (rate cycle) | −0.16pp GVA directly |
| Forest products restructuring | Partly structural (secular paper decline) | Restrains Industry B-E |
| Fiscal austerity (VAT hike, benefit cuts) | Policy choice, cyclical timing | Direct consumption drag |
| Inventory drawdown | Cyclical | −0.53pp |
| ICT sector strength | Structural positive | +0.28pp offset |
| Net exports positive | Cyclical/structural | +0.72pp (only engine) |

The economy is essentially being held together by net exports and ICT sector activity while domestic demand remains suppressed by the interaction of: (a) housing wealth destruction, (b) variable-rate mortgage debt servicing, (c) fiscal tightening, and (d) weak labour market confidence.

## 10. Structural vs. Cyclical Assessment

**CYCLICAL components (expected to normalize 2026–2027):**
- Financial sector GVA compression (NIM will stabilize)
- Inventory cycle drag (seasonal/temporary)
- Household consumption (mortgage relief working through, real wages positive)
- Housing market (stabilizing, construction recovery beginning from low base)

**STRUCTURAL components (persistent):**
- Russia trade severance: permanent level shift in eastern logistics, tourism, wood supply, energy linkages
- Forest/paper industry consolidation: secular decline in graphic paper continues
- Variable-rate mortgage market structure: creates persistent rate sensitivity for Finnish households
- Fiscal consolidation path: rising debt-to-GDP constrains future public spending
- Nordic divergence in industrial structure vs. Denmark's pharma engine

**Overall verdict:** Finland's 2025 weakness is predominantly **CYCLICAL with a structural layer**. The ECB cutting cycle, gradual housing stabilization, and inventory normalization should support a modest recovery in 2026–2027. However, Finland's structural rate sensitivity, Russia legacy, and forest-industry pressures mean the recovery path will be slower and shallower than Sweden's, and the Denmark gap likely reflects durable structural differences that will not close through the cycle.
