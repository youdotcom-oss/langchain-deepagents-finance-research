## 2. German Industrial Coupling

### Finding: Deep structural interdependence; spillover confirmed

Austria's industrial sector is tightly coupled to the German industrial cycle through three main channels: goods export dependence, intermediate-goods supply chains, and capital-goods demand.

Germany accounts for roughly **30% of Austria's goods exports**, making it by far Austria's largest trading partner [[1]]. Critically, intermediate goods to Europe account for approximately **half of Austria's goods exports** [[1]]. When German industrial production weakened sharply across 2023–2025, Austrian intermediate goods suppliers and capital equipment manufacturers were directly exposed.

The IMF's 2025 Article IV for Austria explicitly attributes the 2023–24 recession partly to **weak demand from major trading partners, especially Germany**, noting the downturn was most severe in manufacturing and construction [[1]]. The IMF's spillover analysis finds that weakness in **German intermediate-goods production** carries particularly large spillovers into Austrian intermediate-goods production [[1]].

The OeNB (Austrian National Bank) identifies the structural change in the **European automotive industry** — the accelerating shift toward EVs, falling production volumes at German OEMs, and cost-reduction programmes — as a direct drag on Austrian automotive component suppliers [[3]]. Mechanical engineering and metals together account for roughly **one-fifth of Austrian industrial production (including construction)** and are both highly exposed to German capital-goods demand cycles [[4]].

Austrian manufacturing output fell approximately **12% from its Q4 2022 peak**, with goods exports down roughly **12%** over the same period — figures largely tracking German industrial dynamics [[4]].

**Voestalpine**, Austria's flagship steel company, illustrates the dual squeeze: weakening German/European automotive demand, combined with elevated energy costs and the structural shift in automotive. Reuters reported restructuring actions in Voestalpine's automotive-components business linked to these combined pressures [[9]].

Reuters confirmed German factory activity ended 2024 in **accelerated contraction** (manufacturing PMI below 45), consistent with the continued external-demand headwind facing Austrian industrial suppliers [[2]].

**Assessment:** The German–Austrian industrial linkage is not merely cyclical correlation but a deep structural coupling via intermediate-goods supply chains and capital-equipment demand. As long as German manufacturing remains weak, Austrian Industry B–E will face persistent headwinds. The −0.7% Industry GVA in 2025 (even as GDP turned positive) reflects this coupling remaining active. STRUCTURAL / CYCLICAL hybrid — structural exposure, cyclical intensity.

## 3. Construction Sector Decline (−2.9%)

### Finding: Broad-based residential and commercial correction driven by ECB rate pass-through and Signa-style insolvencies

Austria's construction sector entered a multi-year downturn, with GVA falling for consecutive years from 2022 onward. The −2.9% in 2025 was the continuation of this contraction, dragging −0.17 pp from GDP.

#### (a) Mortgage Rate Pass-Through

Austria historically had a relatively high share of **variable-rate housing loans** compared to other euro-area peers. This made the ECB's tightening cycle particularly impactful. However, by 2024, market behaviour had shifted: OeNB reports that only approximately **one in five new mortgage loans** had a variable rate, meaning new lending had migrated toward fixed-rate products to reduce interest-rate risk [[6]].

The macro effect was nonetheless severe: higher rates sharply reduced housing affordability, compressed mortgage demand, and collapsed residential investment. OeNB states that residential construction investment began falling in **mid-2022** — precisely coinciding with ECB rate hikes — driven by higher financing costs, the end of the housing boom, and deteriorating economic conditions [[5]].

#### (b) Residential Building Permits, 2022–2025

Statistics Austria data confirms a dramatic collapse in the housing pipeline [[7]]:

| Year | Permitted dwellings (new buildings) | All permits incl. conversions |
|------|------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| 2022 | 53,994 | 67,073 |
| 2023 | 37,517 | 48,859 |
| 2024 | 34,422 | 49,443 |
| 2025 | 31,979 | 47,636 |

New dwelling permits fell **41% between 2022 and 2025** — a collapse of historic proportions that will weigh on construction output for years even after demand recovers, given the long lead time between permits and completions.

#### (c) Developer Insolvencies and Commercial Real Estate Stress

Austria experienced severe CRE and developer stress. OeNB's 2025 Financial Stability Report documents nearly **6,600 corporate bankruptcies in 2024**, with **real estate and construction** the hardest-hit sectors. Major insolvencies (liabilities above €10 million) **nearly doubled**, and total liabilities of insolvent companies reached **€18.3 billion** [[8]].

The flagship event was **Signa**, a sprawling Austrian-based real estate conglomerate. Signa Holding — comprising roughly **1,000 companies** — entered insolvency with approximately **€5 billion in debt**. Signa Development also moved into bankruptcy proceedings with a balance sheet of approximately **€4.6 billion** [[9]]. Reuters attributed the collapse to higher interest rates, rising construction costs, frozen bank finance, and a halt in property transactions [[9]].

In response to rising commercial-real-estate NPLs, the OeNB introduced a **sectoral systemic-risk buffer** for CRE lending, effective July 2025 [[8]].

**Assessment:** Austria's construction recession is deep, multi-year, and structural in nature. The collapse in permits means the pipeline will remain thin through at least 2026–2027. Recovery depends primarily on ECB rate normalization (gradual), household affordability restoration, and clearing of CRE distress. STRUCTURAL (prolonged correction cycle, not yet bottomed in starts).

## 4. Energy-Intensive Industries

### Finding: Persistent competitiveness squeeze; no mass closures but sustained output compression

Austria entered the 2022 energy shock with extreme gas exposure: approximately **80% of natural gas was imported from Russia** before 2022 [[11]]. Gas-intensive industries — paper/pulp, chemicals, steel, ceramics — faced an acute competitiveness shock as European gas prices surged.

The IEA documents that value added in Austria's energy-intensive industries remained approximately **7% below 2019 levels** even by 2024–2025, with the most gas- and power-intensive branches the hardest hit [[11]]. This structural output gap reflects both demand-side weakness (German/European industrial cycle) and supply-side cost compression (energy costs eating into margins).

There is **no evidence of a broad wave of permanent Austrian plant closures** attributable solely to energy prices. The dominant pattern is: output compressed, margins squeezed, investment deferred, and restructuring pressure elevated — but Austrian industry adapted rather than collapsed.

Voestalpine — one of Austria's largest single gas consumers — exemplifies the adaptation strategy. Reuters reported that Voestalpine had secured gas supply sufficient for **three to four months** of operations even under a Russian supply cut-off scenario, using international pipeline sources and storage [[10]]. Voestalpine subsequently undertook restructuring in its automotive-components segment, though this reflected a combination of energy costs, labour costs, weak demand, and US tariff risk rather than a single cause [[9]].

Austria's **energy transition progress** is advanced on electricity (renewables covered approximately **90% of electricity demand in 2024**) but gas dependency for space heating and industrial processes remains significant: gas still supplied more than **20% of space-heating demand** in 2024 [[11]]. Austria targets **climate neutrality by 2040** and a **100% renewable annual electricity balance by 2030** [[11]]. The IEA identifies key remaining gaps: wind permitting acceleration, grid expansion, and affordable industrial electricity as critical delivery items [[11]].

**Assessment:** Austria's energy-intensive industry faces a permanent structural shift requiring transition investment and competitiveness adaptation. The near-term squeeze is easing as European gas prices moderate, but the competitiveness challenge relative to US or Asian peers with cheaper energy is structural. STRUCTURAL (medium-term competitiveness challenge).

## 5. Tourism Sector Performance

### Finding: Strong stabilizer — record overnight stays in 2024, supportive in 2025

Tourism was a meaningful positive contributor to Austria's services economy and helped offset manufacturing and construction weakness.

Statistics Austria and Österreich Werbung data show that **Austria recorded approximately 154 million overnight stays in calendar year 2024** — above 2023 and at or near record levels — with tourist arrivals of roughly **47 million**. Germany remained the dominant foreign source market [[4]Q2][16].

The **winter 2024/25 ski season** was described by WIFO (Austrian Economic Research Institute) as **slightly weaker** than the strong prior winter but not contractionary [[14]]. It did not provide a new accelerator but also did not become a recessionary drag.

The **summer 2025 season** was broadly positive in WIFO's tourism analysis, with overnight stays at high levels and foreign demand remaining robust [[5]Q2][7]Q2].

Tourism revenues rose in **nominal terms** in 2025, though part of the gain reflected price increases rather than volume growth — a relevant caveat given 2.8% HICP inflation. In real terms, tourism was a **supportive pillar** rather than the dominant growth engine [[4]Q2].

**Assessment:** Tourism provides a structural cushion for Austria's services economy and helps explain why the back-to-back recessions did not spill over more severely into the services and employment sectors. STRUCTURAL STRENGTH (enduring comparative advantage in tourism).

## 6. Fiscal Policy Response to the 2023–2024 Recession

### Finding: Active support via energy subsidies and social transfers; fiscal deficit widened significantly

Austria did not tighten fiscally into the 2023–24 recession. The government deployed multiple support mechanisms:

- **Energy-price relief** for households and firms, including electricity-cost subsidies and business energy-cost support programmes
- **Cost-of-living transfers** and indexation-linked social benefits reflecting the inflation surge
- **Public investment programmes**, including EU NextGenerationEU / RRF-linked climate and digitalisation projects
- **Housing/construction support** measures reflecting the severity of the residential investment downturn

The IMF's 2025 Article IV materials confirm that fiscal support was active and that fiscal consolidation was necessary once the immediate shock passed [[6]Q2][8]Q2].

Approximate fiscal trajectory:

| Year | General Government Balance | Debt-to-GDP |
|------|---------------------------|------------|
| 2023 | ~−2.5% of GDP | High-70s % |
| 2024 | ~−4.5% to −5.0% of GDP | ~80% of GDP |
| 2025 | Above −3% EU threshold; consolidation beginning | ~80%+ of GDP |

The expenditure data confirms fiscal activism: **Government spending (G) contributed +0.51 pp to GDP growth in 2025**, the largest single positive demand-side contributor. Public-sector GVA (O–Q) grew **+2.8%** (+0.49 pp) — effectively the backbone of Austria's return to positive growth [[6]Q2][8]Q2].

**Assessment:** Fiscal policy successfully cushioned the recession and anchored the 2025 recovery. The cost was a materially wider deficit and an upward drift in debt-to-GDP toward 80%. The fiscal space for further support is constrained, and the consolidation path required in 2026–2027 may act as a mild growth headwind. CYCLICAL (support measures temporary, consolidation path now under way).

## 7. Labour Market Conditions

### Finding: Resilient despite stagnation — unemployment rose only modestly; real wages recovered

Austria's labour market absorbed two years of contraction and a modest recovery with only moderate deterioration, in part because of Austria's institutionalised **collective bargaining** wage-setting system.

Approximate unemployment trajectory:

| Year | Unemployment Rate (Eurostat) |
|------|------------------------------|
| 2023 | ~5.1% |
| 2024 | ~5.2% |
| 2025 | ~5.7% |

The rise in unemployment was modest by historical recession standards — reflecting labour hoarding by firms, short-time work (Kurzarbeit) arrangements in manufacturing and construction, and the continued expansion of public-sector employment [[9]Q2][10]Q2].

**Wage dynamics** were a key factor in the 2025 consumption recovery. Austria's collectively bargained wages are **backward-looking**, indexing to prior-year inflation. With inflation running at ~8–9% in 2022–23, wage settlements in 2024 were very strong. As inflation dropped toward 2.8% by 2025, these elevated wages translated into meaningful **real wage gains** — directly supporting private consumption's +0.24 pp contribution to GDP growth [[9]Q2][10]Q2][13]Q2].

Short-time work did not return at pandemic scale, but became more relevant in weak industrial sectors (metals, machinery, construction-related trades). OeNB's 2025 outlooks highlight continued labour market softening, noting that rising unemployment reflects a genuine, if mild, weakening rather than just a statistical artefact [[9]Q2][10]Q2].

**Assessment:** Austria's labour market institutional structure (collective bargaining, Kurzarbeit) acted as an automatic stabilizer, limiting unemployment increases and preserving household income through the recession. This partly explains why private consumption did not contract sharply. STRUCTURAL STRENGTH (institutional shock absorber).

## 8. Is the +0.6% Reading a Genuine Turning Point?

### Assessment: Tentative stabilization, not a self-sustaining recovery

The +0.6% real GDP growth in 2025 marks Austria's first positive annual reading after two years of contraction. The evidence suggests this is a **genuine but fragile turning point** — not a bounce that will mechanically fade, but equally not yet a self-sustaining private-sector upswing.

**Positive signals:**
- GDP returned to positive growth across all four quarters
- Private consumption contributed positively, supported by real wage recovery
- GFCF turned mildly positive (+0.32 pp), suggesting investment stabilisation
- Tourism remained strong; real-estate GVA was positive (+2.7%)
- Inventory rebuilding (ΔInv +0.30 pp) added cyclical support

**Fragility indicators:**
- The largest single growth driver was **government spending** (+0.51 pp G; +0.49 pp public-sector GVA) — a temporary rather than sustainable engine
- Unemployment **still rose** to ~5.7% in 2025, signalling the labour market had not yet stabilised
- Construction GVA was still **contracting** (−2.9%); no housing-starts recovery yet
- Industry B–E remained in **contraction** (−0.7%), with German demand still weak
- Net exports dragged **−0.75 pp** — Austria is not yet benefiting from an external demand upswing

**Forecasts from IMF, OeNB, and WIFO** for 2026 are uniformly modest: approximately **+1% real GDP growth** rather than a sharp cyclical rebound [[3]Q2][6]Q2][8]Q2][9]Q2][10]Q2][13]Q2].

**Key conditions for a genuine recovery:**
1. **German demand recovery** — Austria cannot sustain broad industrial growth without it
2. **ECB rate normalization** — lower rates must filter into mortgage affordability and construction restarts
3. **Construction pipeline rebuild** — permit collapse of 2022–2025 will weigh through 2026–2027 absent demand recovery
4. **Fiscal consolidation managed carefully** — necessary for EU compliance but risks subtracting from domestic demand
5. **Energy competitiveness restoration** — ongoing transition costs and industrial restructuring must stabilise

**Bottom line:** Austria is emerging from its recession but on fragile foundations. The recovery is being led by the public sector and real wage income effects rather than by export demand or private investment. 2026 represents the test of whether the private sector can take over from fiscal impulse as the growth engine — and that depends critically on developments in Germany.

## 9. Synthesis: Structural vs. Cyclical Assessment

| Phenomenon | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| German industrial coupling / export weakness | Structural exposure + cyclical intensity |
| Construction recession (permits, insolvencies) | Structural (multi-year correction, 2026–27 overhang) |
| Energy-intensive industry competitiveness | Structural (medium-term transition challenge) |
| Tourism as services cushion | Structural strength (enduring) |
| Fiscal expansion into deficit | Cyclical (consolidation now beginning) |
| Labour market resilience | Structural strength (institutional) |
| +0.6% GDP in 2025 | Tentative turning point — fragile, public-sector led |

Austria's back-to-back 2023–2024 contractions were the product of **three simultaneous structural shocks**: (1) the German industrial downturn transmitted via supply chains; (2) the construction and real estate correction triggered by the fastest ECB tightening cycle in decades; and (3) the energy-price squeeze on energy-intensive industry. The 2025 recovery is real but narrow, anchored in fiscal expansion and real wage income recovery rather than in a broad private-sector rebound. A durable return to pre-2022 growth rates requires resolution or stabilisation of all three structural issues simultaneously — and the German demand channel in particular remains the dominant uncertainty.
