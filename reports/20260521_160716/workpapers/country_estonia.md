## 2. The Russia Transit Shock: Structural Collapse in Transport/Logistics

### Pre-War Scale of Russia-Linked Transit
Estonia was one of the EU's most Russia-exposed transit economies. Pre-2022, Estonia handled approximately **25 million tonnes per year** of Russian refined products via Tallinn/Muuga, representing roughly one quarter of Russia's refined-product exports, plus coal, metals, timber, and chemicals transit [[1]]. Port of Tallinn reported that Russian-origin liquid bulk and fertilisers had averaged approximately **one-third of its total cargo throughput** in normal years, with Belarusian-origin liquid bulk adding another **9% of 2021 throughput** [[2]].

### The Collapse in Freight Volumes
The sanctions shock after February 2022 triggered a cascade of cargo losses that has proven permanent rather than temporary:

**Port of Tallinn (Tallinna Sadam) annual cargo throughput:**
| Year | Total Cargo (mt) | Change vs. 2021 |
|------|-----------------|----------------|
| 2021 | 22.397 | — |
| 2022 | 17.761 | −4.636 mt (−20.7%) |
| 2023 | 12.586 | −9.811 mt (−43.8%) |
| 2024 | 13.134 | −9.263 mt (−41.4%) |
| 2025 | ~14.0 | ~−37% below 2021 |

[[3]][[4]][[6]]

**The core Russia-transit cargo effectively ceased:**
- Liquid bulk fell from **8.837 million tonnes in 2021** to **1.362 million tonnes in 2024** — a loss of **7.475 million tonnes, or −84.6%** [[4]][[3]]
- Combined liquid bulk + dry bulk fell from **13.530 mt (2021)** to **3.943 mt (2024)** — a loss of **9.587 mt, or −70.9%** [[4]][[3]]

**Rail freight was hit even harder than ports:**
- Rail transit goods fell from **9.5 mt in 2021** → **3.8 mt in 2022** → **2.1 mt in 2023** — a **−77.9% cumulative drop** [[5]]
- Statistics Estonia reported national rail freight fell **−43% in 2023** alone, to just **10.1 million tonnes** [[5]]

**Estonian Railways (Elron) is now structurally unviable on freight:**
- Lost **€30.7 million in 2024**; expected losses of **€35 million in 2025** [[7]]
- Only **3 million tonnes** of freight on the national network in 2024 [[7]]
- Officials state breakeven requires **20–25 million tonnes per year** — now described as "unreachable" after the end of Russian oil transit [[7]]

### Link to the G-I GVA Collapse
Eesti Pank's 2025 monetary policy review explicitly identifies the fall in **transport and storage** value added as partly a consequence of Russia's war in Ukraine and sanctions, and notes the deterioration in relations with Russia makes recovery in that sector unlikely in the near term [[8]]. The replacement cargoes being developed (containers, ro-ro, dry bulk from non-Russian sources) are smaller-volume and unable to support the cost base built around high-volume Russian oil/fertiliser transit.

The −7.5% GVA collapse in G-I in 2025 therefore reflects: (a) the multi-year unwinding of Russia-linked logistics contracts that had propped up transport revenue through 2023; (b) weakness in retail trade from consumer spending contraction; and (c) the broader competitiveness shock feeding through into service-sector demand.

**Assessment: STRUCTURAL.** The Russia transit routes are not returning. Rail freight losses are permanent at current volumes. Port of Tallinn has stabilised at ~14 mt/year (~60% of 2021 levels) but liquid bulk is unlikely to recover to pre-2022 levels.

## 3. Inflation: Tax-Driven Services Spiral at 4.8% HICP

### Component Breakdown of Estonia's HICP (2025 Annual Average)
| Component | 2025 Rate |
|-----------|----------|
| All-items HICP | **4.8%** |
| Services | **9.6%** |
| Food (incl. alcohol & tobacco) | **6.5%** |
| Non-energy industrial goods | **1.1%** |
| Energy | **−2.9%** |
| Electricity | **−4.2%** |

[[2_inf]][[3_inf]]

*(Note: Romania at 6.8% was higher within the EU-27; Estonia was among the highest in the eurozone.)*

### What Explains the Persistence?

**1. Tax hikes as a mechanical driver (~2pp impact)**
Estonia implemented major fiscal consolidation measures in 2025:
- Vehicle registration tax (new in 2025): contributed **+1.2pp** to headline inflation [[4_inf]]
- VAT increase to 24% (from 22%): contributed **+0.7pp** [[4_inf]]
- Higher personal and corporate income tax rates (raised to 22%) and excise increases [[5_inf]]
- Without these tax effects, Eesti Pank estimates underlying inflation would have been approximately **3%** [[4_inf]]

**2. Services inflation — the stickiest component (9.6%)**
Services inflation averaged 9.6% for 2025 [[2_inf]]. In May 2025, Eesti Pank reported services inflation of **10% y/y**, with administered-price effects contributing **4.4pp** (car tax, higher medical/hospital fees); leisure services added further pressure — package holidays +20%, hotels +12% [[6_inf]].

**3. Wage-price feedback loop**
The IMF and Eesti Pank both identify a second-round effect: nominal wage growth of **~8.4% in 2025** is feeding into labour-intensive services prices [[7_inf]][[8_inf]]. The IMF explicitly notes that tax increases may have fueled wage demands, which then passed through into services prices where labour is a large input cost [[8_inf]].

**4. Food inflation (6.5%)**
Eesti Pank attributes food inflation to global commodity price pass-through into retail prices, with meat products (chicken, beef) notably elevated [[4_inf]].

**5. Energy was volatile but not the primary driver on an annual basis**
Despite annual energy HICP of −2.9%, electricity prices were episodically high (~€150/MWh in February 2025, falling to ~€56/MWh by June, rising again to ~€100/MWh in November) [[6_inf]][[9_inf]].

**Assessment: PARTLY CYCLICAL.** The tax-driven component (~2pp) was a one-off level shift; as the base effects wash out in 2026, headline inflation should moderate. However, services inflation at 9.6% driven by wage dynamics is more persistent and represents a wage-price dynamic that will take longer to resolve.

## 4. Real Wages and Household Purchasing Power

Despite high inflation, nominal wage growth has outpaced prices, creating positive real wages — but this has not yet translated into a consumption recovery:

| Year | Nominal Wage Growth | HICP Inflation | Approx. Real Wage Growth |
|------|--------------------|--------------|-----------------------|
| 2024 | ~8.1% | ~3.7% | ~+4.2% |
| 2025 | ~8.4% | 4.8% | ~+3.4% |

[[10_q2]][[5_inf]]

However, **household consumption remains subdued** (expenditure decomposition: C = −0.07pp). The IMF expected private consumption near-stagnation in 2024–2025 before a clearer recovery in 2026 [[10_q2]]. The European Commission confirms private consumption is recovering only slowly: consumer confidence remains fragile, recent tax increases have reduced disposable income, and households are cautious after the cumulative 2022–2023 inflation shock [[14_q2]].

The disconnect between positive real wages and weak consumption likely reflects: (a) elevated savings rates as households rebuild cushions; (b) high mortgage costs from ECB rate hikes (Estonia's variable-rate mortgage exposure); and (c) debt service pressures.

## 5. Why Estonia Has Lagged Latvia (+2.1%) and Lithuania (+2.9%)

The Baltic divergence is explained by several reinforcing factors:

**a) Deeper starting recession**
Estonia entered 2025 having contracted in each year from 2022–2024 (−1.2%, −2.7%, −0.1%). On a 2021=100 index basis, Estonia's GDP path is roughly **3–4% below 2021**, while Latvia and Lithuania recovered faster [[14_q2]].

**b) Greater Russia transit exposure (structural, not just cyclical)**
Estonia's logistics and transport sector was disproportionately built around Russian transit. The −77.9% rail transit collapse and −84.6% liquid bulk port decline are uniquely severe within the Baltics. Latvia and Lithuania faced similar shocks but had less port/rail dependence on Russian oil-product transit specifically.

**c) Nordic export exposure**
Estonia is unusually exposed to Finnish and Swedish industrial, construction and real-estate cycles — sectors that were depressed by high Nordic interest rates in 2023–2024 [[14_q2]]. This suppressed demand for Estonian manufacturing exports (wood products, building materials, furniture, modular construction). Lithuania and Latvia have more diversified export mixes.

**d) Competitiveness damage from wage-inflation spiral**
Wages have risen faster than productivity, eroding Estonia's export cost competitiveness. The IMF specifically flags this as a binding constraint on recovery [[10_q2]]. Estonia's wage growth of ~8% in both 2024 and 2025 has not been matched by productivity gains.

**e) Energy system and oil shale legacy**
Estonia's oil-shale-based energy system made the 2022 energy price shock particularly acute, imposing higher input costs on industry that have not fully normalized.

**f) Fiscal policy was contractionary in 2025**
Estonia implemented significant tax increases in 2025 (VAT, income tax, vehicle tax) as part of fiscal consolidation, reducing disposable income exactly when the economy needed consumer support. Latvia and Lithuania had less contractionary near-term fiscal stances.

## 6. Government Fiscal and Defence Spending

### Fiscal Position
The European Commission projects Estonia's general government deficit at:
| Year | Deficit (% GDP) | Public Debt (% GDP) |
|------|----------------|---------------------|
| 2025 | −1.3% | ~23.4% |
| 2026 | −4.4% | ~25.9% |
| 2027 | −4.4% | ~29.2% |

[[14_q2]]

### Defence Spending Surge
Defence is driving a major fiscal expansion:
- Defence expenditure is rising from approximately **3.7–3.8% of GDP in 2024–2025** to approximately **5.1% of GDP in 2026** [[3_q2]]
- Eesti Pank estimates the additional fiscal injection in 2026 (vs. 2025) at approximately **€1.1 billion, or ~2.5% of GDP** — explicitly tied to defence spending [[6_q2]]
- Eesti Pank stated the 2026 growth outlook comes "largely from **borrowed government money**" [[6_q2]]

### Revenue-Side (2025): Tax Tightening
To fund 2025 consolidation, Estonia raised:
- VAT: 22% → 24%
- Personal and corporate income tax: raised to 22%
- New vehicle registration (motor car) tax
- Total revenue measures estimated at **~1.1% of GDP** [[14_q2]]

### Fiscal Multiplier Constraints
IMF analysis puts Estonia's first-year government spending multipliers at approximately **0.6–1.2** for aggregate spending, but defence procurement carries a significantly lower domestic multiplier due to **high import content** (equipment sourced from NATO suppliers abroad) [[8_q2]]. The positive demand effect of defence spending will therefore be partly offset by import leakage.

## 7. Structural Outlook: Turning Point or Further Weakness?

### Forecast Consensus
| Forecaster | 2025F | 2026F | 2027F |
|------------|-------|-------|-------|
| European Commission | +0.6% | +2.1% | +2.0% |
| Eesti Pank (Sept. 2025) | +0.6% | >+3.0% | — |
| IMF (2025 Article IV) | +0.6% | ~+2.0% | — |

[[14_q2]][[6_q2]][[8_q2]]

### Why 2025 Is Likely the Trough
The recovery has identifiable drivers:
1. **Positive real wages** (~+3.4% in 2025) will eventually translate into consumption — households are cautious but have growing incomes
2. **Defence spending** (~5.1% of GDP in 2026) will provide a material fiscal impulse, even with import leakage
3. **ECB rate cuts** (ongoing since 2024) reduce mortgage and credit costs, supporting housing and investment
4. **Base effects** — after four years of negative or near-zero growth, the growth denominator is very low
5. **Port of Tallinn stabilisation** at ~14 mt/year and partial diversification to non-Russian cargoes

### Persistent Risks
1. **Transport/logistics remains structurally impaired** — rail freight losses are permanent; no return to pre-2022 transit volumes
2. **Competitiveness** — wage growth outpacing productivity is a lasting problem without complementary productivity-enhancing investment
3. **Nordic demand** — if Finland and Sweden remain weak, Estonian manufacturing exports continue to underperform
4. **Fiscal sustainability** — deficit projected at −4.4% of GDP in 2026–2027; public debt rising quickly from a low base, but trajectory warrants monitoring
5. **Services inflation persistence** — 9.6% services HICP suggests the wage-price dynamic is not resolved

**Assessment:** Estonia is at a **policy-supported turning point** driven primarily by government and defence spending. The 2026 acceleration to ~2.1% growth is plausible but is **fiscal- rather than private-sector-led**. A genuinely self-sustaining recovery requires: (a) normalisation of services inflation to allow household confidence to recover; (b) Nordic demand revival; and (c) longer-term reorientation of the transport/logistics sector away from the now-lost Russia transit model.

## 8. Summary Assessment

| Dimension | Assessment | Structural/Cyclical |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| G-I sector collapse | Russia transit permanently lost; rail unviable | **STRUCTURAL** |
| Inflation at 4.8% | ~2pp from one-off taxes; 9.6% services more persistent | **MIXED** |
| Real wage/consumption gap | Positive real wages but cautious households | **CYCLICAL** |
| Baltic underperformance | Deeper Russia exposure + Nordic cycle + cost competitiveness | **PARTLY STRUCTURAL** |
| Recovery trajectory | 2026 fiscal-driven uptick likely, not private-sector led | **CYCLICAL TROUGH** |

**Overall verdict:** Estonia's extraordinary GDP underperformance (worst in EU-27, 2022–2025) reflects a permanent structural shock — the destruction of its Russia-linked transit economy, which accounted for up to one-third of port throughput and was the backbone of rail freight — compounded by cyclical headwinds (Nordic demand weakness, ECB rate hikes, energy price shock). The 2025 turning point is real but fragile. Full recovery to pre-2022 levels is unlikely without: (a) alternative large-scale freight flows replacing the lost Russian transit, (b) Nordic construction/industrial revival, and (c) resolution of the wage-competitiveness problem.
