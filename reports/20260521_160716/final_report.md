# EU-27 GDP Analysis 2025: Anomalies, Industry Drivers, and Macroeconomic Trends

**Prepared:** 21 May 2026  
**Scope:** All 27 EU member states — 2025 real GDP growth, anomaly detection, sector/expenditure decomposition, and country-level investigation  
**Data vintage:** Eurostat releases through 20 May 2026 (GDP, HICP) and 15 April 2026 (Current Account)

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The EU-27 economy grew at an unweighted average of **2.2% in 2025** (median 1.8%), but this headline masks a deeply fractured landscape. **Ireland's +12.3% reading** — a statistical artefact of multinational pharmaceutical export surges ahead of US tariffs — is the sole formal anomaly, inflating the EU average by nearly half a percentage point. Excluding Ireland, the mean drops to **1.7%**.

At the bottom, **Germany and Finland** both recorded just **+0.2%**, with Germany completing a third consecutive year of near-zero or negative growth — its weakest sustained stretch since reunification. A cluster of manufacturing-dependent economies (Austria, Hungary) and Russia-exposed northern economies (Estonia, Finland) round out the laggards. At the top, **Malta (+4.0%) and Cyprus (+3.8%)** continue to outperform on tourism, ICT services, and migration-driven demand.

The most important structural finding is the **collapse of the German industrial growth model**: cheap Russian energy, strong Chinese demand, and premium combustion-engine exports are all simultaneously impaired. This radiates through EU value chains, dragging Austria, Hungary, and the broader European capital-goods cycle. Meanwhile, services-oriented periphery economies and CEE convergence economies continue to grow at or above trend, creating a widening two-speed Europe.

## 2. Methodology & Data Notes

### Data Sources
- **GDP growth**: Eurostat `nama_10_gdp`, chain-linked volumes (% change on previous year), vintage 2026-05-20 [[1]]
- **HICP inflation**: Eurostat `PRC_HICP_AINR`, annual average rate of change, vintage 2026-05-20 [[2]]
- **Current account**: Eurostat `BOP_GDP6_Q`, % of GDP, vintage 2026-04-15 [[3]]
- **Expenditure decomposition**: Eurostat `nama_10_gdp`, pp contributions (`CON_PPCH_PRE`), vintage 2026-05-20 [[4]]
- **Sector GVA**: Eurostat `nama_10_a10`, chain-linked volumes and pp contributions, vintage 2026-04-23 [[5]]
- **Country investigations**: IMF Article IV reports, national central banks (Bundesbank, Bank of Finland, Eesti Pank, MNB, OeNB, STATEC, CBM), CSO Ireland, Reuters, Bloomberg, European Commission forecasts [[6]–[[50]]

### Key Caveats
- **Ireland GDP vs GNI***: Ireland's headline GDP is distorted by MNC activity. Modified GNI (GNI*) is the appropriate welfare measure; the 2025 gap is approximately **9.7 percentage points** [[6]][[7]].
- **Base effects**: Deep 2020 contractions mechanically inflated 2021 rebounds (Croatia +12.6%, Cyprus +11.4%, Malta +13.4%). By 2025, these effects have largely washed out.
- **2025 data vintage**: For several member states, 2025 annual data is a first-release estimate subject to revision.
- **Current account discrepancies**: Minor differences (1–2pp) exist between Eurostat BOP data and IMF/national-source figures cited in country workpapers, reflecting different data vintages and seasonal adjustment methods. Eurostat figures from the landscape scan are used as the primary reference.

## 6. Anomaly Analysis — High Growth

### 🔴 Ireland (+12.3%) — "The MNC Mirage"

**Classification: Primarily CYCLICAL spike on a STRUCTURAL base**

Ireland's headline GDP growth is almost entirely disconnected from Irish living standards. The +12.3% figure was driven by a **tariff-triggered pharmaceutical export surge**: US-based multinationals with Irish manufacturing operations front-loaded exports ahead of anticipated US tariff implementations in early 2025.

**Key evidence:**
- February 2025 alone saw **€10.5bn in pharma/medical exports** to the US — up **450%+ year-on-year** [[8]]
- The **GLP-1/peptide-hormone subsector** (diabetes/obesity drugs, primarily Eli Lilly's tirzepatide products) was the most clearly identified driver [[9]]
- **GFCF contributed +6.32pp** and **inventory accumulation +4.40pp** — consistent with MNC intangible-asset investment and supply-chain stockpiling [[4]]
- **Private consumption was negative (−0.32pp)** — Irish households were absent from this "boom" [[4]]

**GDP vs. GNI* divergence:**

| Measure | 2025 Growth |
|---|---|
| Real GDP | +12.3% |
| Real GNP | +2.4% |
| Real GNI* (est.) | ~+2.5% |
| Modified Domestic Demand | +4.9% |

The GDP–GNI* gap of ~9.7pp reflects **€56.8bn in factor income outflows** (profit repatriations), up 42.2% year-on-year [[6]]. GNI* stood at only **54–56% of headline GDP** in level terms.

**Sector decomposition:** Industry excl. construction (B-E) grew **+29.1%** (manufacturing +31.0%), contributing **+6.55pp** to GDP — almost entirely pharma/chemicals. ICT (J) added +1.53pp (+11.5% growth). Professional services (M-N) contracted −4.0% [[5]].

**Fiscal paradox:** Ireland runs a **€12.4bn surplus (3.7% of GNI*)**, but **88% of corporation tax is paid by foreign MNCs**, with the top 3 firms contributing 38% of all receipts [[10]]. The housing crisis persists (~35,000 completions vs. ~50,000 needed) despite the fiscal windfall.

**Outlook:** Significant GDP deceleration in 2026 is virtually certain as inventories reverse and front-loading fades. Underlying GNI* trajectory (~2.5%) is the reliable forward indicator.

---

### 🟡 Malta (+4.0%) — Tourism-Services Hub

**Classification: STRUCTURAL model with CYCLICAL normalisation**

Malta's deceleration from 13.4% (2021) to 4.0% (2025) is healthy normalisation, not weakness. Growth is driven by three structural pillars:

1. **Tourism** — Trade/transport/accommodation (G-I) grew +8.8% (+1.49pp), the single largest contributor [[5]]. Malta receives >5 tourists per resident in peak years [[11]].
2. **iGaming/ICT** — The Malta Gaming Authority licensing model has created a dominant EU online gambling hub, contributing ~12% of GVA (direct + indirect). ICT sector grew +6.9% (+0.81pp) [[12]].
3. **Migration-driven labour expansion** — Labour force grew from ~281k (2021) to ~320k (2025), expanding aggregate GDP through labour-input growth [[11]].

**Expenditure:** Broadly based — C +1.50pp, G +1.00pp, NX +1.04pp [[4]].

**Risks:** High tourism concentration (COVID demonstrated deep vulnerability), housing affordability stress from migration-driven demand, iGaming regulatory headwinds, and flat GFCF suggesting insufficient capital deepening.

---

### 🟡 Cyprus (+3.8%) — Diversifying Services Economy

**Classification: STRUCTURAL transition with cyclical amplification**

Cyprus is the only country where **all sectors recorded positive GVA growth** in 2025 [[5]]. Growth is broad-based:

- **Tourism/trade** (G-I): +6.4% (+1.31pp) — visitor volumes back to or above the pre-pandemic 4 million benchmark [[13]]
- **ICT sector**: +8.0% (+0.83pp) — Cyprus has become a genuine tech hub anchored by gaming companies (Wargaming, GDEV/Nexters), fintech (Exness, FxPro), and post-2022 Russian/Ukrainian tech firm relocations [[13]]
- **Net services exports**: The largest expenditure driver at +2.45pp [[4]]

**Low inflation paradox:** HICP of just 0.8% (EU's lowest) despite solid growth, explained by energy disinflation and growth coming through net exports rather than domestic demand overheating [[2]][[13]].

**Vulnerability:** The **−6.4% current account deficit** [[3]] is chronic and driven by structural goods imports, partially offset by services surpluses. Not crisis-level given FDI financing, but warrants monitoring.

## 7. Anomaly Analysis — Low Growth / Contraction

### 🟡 Germany (+0.2%) — "The Industrial Patient"

**Classification: STRUCTURAL stagnation — the post-reunification growth model is broken**

Germany's three-year trajectory of −0.9%, −0.5%, +0.2% (2023–2025) is unprecedented in the post-reunification era outside of crisis years. The root cause is the **simultaneous collapse of three pillars** of the German growth model:

1. **Cheap Russian energy** → Permanently gone. Industrial electricity at **€150–200/MWh vs. ~$70–90/MWh in the US** [[14]]. Chemical plant utilisation at a **20-year low**; BASF restructuring Ludwigshafen; Ineos cutting German output [[15]].

2. **Chinese export demand** → Structurally impaired. BYD and SAIC/MG are displacing German automakers in both the Chinese domestic market and European imports. China was the **primary profit pool** for German OEMs; domestic Chinese brands are eroding it through vertically integrated supply chains [[16]].

3. **Premium combustion-engine exports** → Disrupted by EV transition. **VW considering at least 3 German plant closures** — unprecedented in post-war history. Mercedes-Benz retreating from EV timelines; BMW profits under pressure [[17]].

**Expenditure:** Consumption (+0.82pp) was the only meaningful support. Net exports **subtracted −1.53pp** — the export model is broken. GFCF was essentially zero (−0.04pp) [[4]].

**Sector:** Construction (−2.9%) and financial services (−3.6%) were the biggest drags. Manufacturing contracted −1.0%. Only public administration (+1.5%) provided measurable positive support [[5]].

**Policy response:** The **March 2025 constitutional amendment** created a **€500bn infrastructure fund** and exempted defence spending above 1% GDP from the debt brake — the largest German fiscal regime shift in a generation [[18]]. But execution capacity (permitting, planning, administrative bandwidth) is the binding constraint, not financing.

**Spillovers:** Germany's weakness propagates directly to Austria (−0.7% in 2024, +0.6% in 2025) and Hungary (+0.5%) through automotive and industrial supply chains, and depresses the broader EU capital-goods cycle [[19]].

---

### 🟡 Finland (+0.2%) — "The Nordic Laggard"

**Classification: Primarily CYCLICAL with a STRUCTURAL layer**

Finland's near-stagnation results from **five overlapping pressures**:

1. **Russia trade severance** — A permanent level shift, not an ongoing drag. Lost cheap energy, Russian roundwood for mills, transit logistics, and tourist flows mean Finland operates from a permanently lower GDP base [[20]].

2. **Financial sector collapse (−4.6%)** — NIM compression as ECB rate cuts reversed the abnormally high net interest income banks (Nordea, OP Group) earned during the 2022–2023 hiking cycle. Cyclical, not a credit crisis [[21]].

3. **Forestry/pulp/paper structural decline** — UPM, Stora Enso, and Metsä Group face secular graphic paper demand decline plus loss of cheap Russian roundwood [[20]].

4. **Variable-rate mortgage pass-through** — Finland's **~90% variable-rate mortgage market** means ECB hikes passed directly into household cash flows, suppressing consumption (−0.12pp). Even with ECB cuts underway, relief is lagged [[22]].

5. **Fiscal austerity** — The Orpo government implemented spending cuts and a **VAT hike to 25.5%** during a housing-led recession, directly compounding domestic demand weakness [[23]].

**Nordic divergence:** Sweden (+1.5%) has a more diversified economy and no equivalent Russia exposure. Denmark (+2.9%) benefits from the **Novo Nordisk/pharma export engine** — a GDP-level statistical concentration effect similar to Ireland's, inflating Danish headline numbers [[24]].

**Expenditure:** Net exports (+0.72pp) was the only genuine growth driver. Both consumption and government spending were slightly negative [[4]].

---

### 🟡 Estonia (+0.6%) — "The Baltic Attrition"

**Classification: STRUCTURAL level shift from Russia shock + CYCLICAL household compression**

Estonia has the **worst cumulative GDP trajectory in the EU-27** from 2022–2025: −1.2%, −2.7%, −0.1%, +0.6% — a net decline of approximately **−3.4%** over four years. No other EU economy has absorbed a multi-year contraction of this magnitude in the post-GFC period.

**The Russia transit collapse is structural and permanent:**
- Port of Tallinn cargo fell from **22.4 mt (2021) to 13.1 mt (2024)**, still −41% below 2021 [[25]]
- Liquid bulk (Russian oil products) fell **−84.6%** from 2021 to 2024 [[25]]
- Estonian Railways lost €30.7m in 2024 and expects €35m losses in 2025, with breakeven described as **"unreachable"** [[26]]
- Trade/transport/accommodation (G-I) collapsed **−7.5%** in 2025 (−1.20pp) — the single largest sectoral drag relative to GDP size among all investigated countries [[5]]

**Inflation:** HICP of **4.8%** (EU's highest) is partly tax-driven — government VAT hikes (22→24%), new vehicle tax, and income tax rises mechanically added ~2pp. Without tax effects, underlying inflation would be ~3% [[27]]. A wage-price spiral in services (nominal wages ~8.4%) accounts for the rest.

**Baltic divergence:** Estonia underperforms Latvia (+2.1%) and Lithuania (+2.9%) due to (a) deeper Russia transit exposure, (b) greater Nordic construction/real-estate export dependence, (c) contractionary fiscal stance, (d) oil-shale energy system vulnerability, and (e) wages outpacing productivity [[27]].

**Outlook:** Defence spending rises to **~5.1% of GDP in 2026**, injecting ~€1.1bn in additional fiscal stimulus. But defence procurement has high import leakage, limiting the domestic multiplier. Recovery is **policy-supported, not private-sector-led** [[28]].

---

### 🟡 Italy (+0.5%) — "The Slow Deceleration"

**Classification: STRUCTURAL stagnation with fading cyclical tailwinds**

Italy's trajectory from +8.9% (2021) to +0.5% (2025) reflects the complete unwinding of post-COVID bounce effects, leaving the economy at its **structural growth floor**. The IMF projects medium-term growth at ~0.7%, citing a "double-digit" decline in working-age population by 2050 and negative annual labour-productivity growth [[29]].

**The Superbonus fiscal legacy:** The construction tax incentive, originally projected at €35bn, ultimately cost ~**€160bn in ~4 years** [[30]]. Debt/GDP rises from 137.1% (2025) to a peak of 138.6% in 2026 as deferred tax-credit cash flows crystallise. The +2.4% construction growth in 2025 is a tail/work-in-progress effect that will fade.

**Manufacturing weakness:** Stellantis Italian car output fell to **213,706 units in 2025 — the lowest since 1954** [[31]]. Industrial production fell 4.0% in 2024. Italian industrial electricity at **278 €/MWh vs. 171 €/MWh in Spain** is a structural cost handicap [[32]].

**NRRP absorption:** Italy has secured ~**€166bn (86.7%)** of its €191.5bn NextGenerationEU allocation, making it one of the EU's most advanced recipients. But the end-2026 deadline creates last-mile execution risk [[33]].

**Labour paradox:** Employment is growing (+1.7% YoY), unemployment at 6.5% — but this masks **zero productivity growth**. Italy generates output by adding workers, not by increasing productivity, which is demographically unsustainable [[29]].

---

### 🟡 Hungary (+0.5%) — "The Stagflationary Trap"

**Classification: CYCLICAL in origin but held in place by STRUCTURAL constraints**

Hungary presents a **stagflationary configuration**: below-average growth (+0.5%) with above-average inflation (4.4%) — the worst combination in the EU-27.

**Manufacturing drag (−2.5% GVA, −0.47pp):** Driven by weak German/EU end-demand through automotive supply chains (Audi Győr, Mercedes Kecskemét, BMW Debrecen). EV transition timing mismatch: BYD and BMW plants were not yet ramping in 2025 [[34]].

**Inflation persistence (4.4%):** Five overlapping channels — food-price stickiness, HUF depreciation pass-through, energy costs, services repricing, and fragile inflation expectations. The MNB held rates at **6.5%** throughout 2025 as HUF weakness constrained easing [[35]].

**EU funds freeze:** ~**€19–20bn still blocked** over rule-of-law conditionality — the key explanation for GFCF contraction (−0.65pp) despite large announced FDI [[36]].

**Consumption (+1.60pp)** was the sole meaningful growth driver, reflecting genuine real-wage recovery after the 2022–23 inflation shock. But sustainability is conditional on inflation stabilising. Net exports subtracted a heavy **−1.70pp** [[4]].

---

### 🟡 Austria (+0.6%) — "Germany's Shadow"

**Classification: STRUCTURAL coupling to Germany + CYCLICAL construction downturn**

Austria is the only EU country besides Germany to record **back-to-back contractions** in 2023 (−0.8%) and 2024 (−0.7%). The +0.6% recovery in 2025 is the weakest positive print in the post-COVID period.

**German coupling:** Germany absorbs ~30% of Austrian goods exports. OeNB analysis links weak Austrian growth directly to soft German demand [[37]]. Austrian manufacturing output fell **~12% from its Q4 2022 peak**, tracking German industrial contraction closely.

**Construction crisis (−2.9%):** Building permits collapsed **41% from 53,994 (2022) to 31,979 (2025)** [[38]]. Signa Holding (Austria's flagship real estate group) went insolvent with ~€5bn in debt [[39]]. The pipeline collapse will overhang construction output through at least 2026–27.

**Growth engine:** Public administration/health (+2.8%, +0.49pp) was the largest contributor, meaning recovery is **fiscally anchored, not market-driven** [[5]]. Tourism (~154 million overnight stays in 2024) provides a meaningful services cushion [[37]].

---

### 🟡 Luxembourg (+0.6%) — "The Financial Centre on Pause"

**Classification: STRUCTURAL — new lower growth equilibrium**

Luxembourg's four years of near-stagnation (cumulative ~0% real GDP 2022–2025) reflect a **concentrated financial economy** hit precisely where it was most vulnerable.

**Fund industry:** AUM fell from ~€5.86tn (end-2021) to ~€5.03tn (end-2022) through mark-to-market losses during the ECB hiking cycle. Recovery to ~€6tn by 2025 has not fully translated into GDP because value-added depends on fees and employment, not just AUM [[40]]. Financial sector GVA grew only +1.1% in 2025.

**Industry collapse (−6.7%, −0.35pp):** A wide industrial recession across energy-intensive manufacturing, construction-linked materials, and automotive components — downstream from the construction crash (−40% vs. pre-pandemic) [[41]].

**Housing:** Europe's most expensive market fell **16–20% from Q3 2022 peak**, but affordability did not improve — doubled mortgage rates more than offset price declines [[40]].

**MNC statistical artefact:** The 2024 gross trade flows (EX −26.48pp, IM +25.19pp) illustrate how Luxembourg's financial-hub SPE transactions produce wild statistical noise. Unlike Ireland, Luxembourg publishes **no official modified national income measure** [[40]].

**Outlook:** STATEC projects +2.0% in 2026, ~2.4% average 2027–2029 [[41]]. The pre-2022 growth model (rapid commuter inflow + strong finance + rising real estate) looks structurally impaired. A new trend closer to ~2% is more realistic than a return to the 4–5% rates of the prior decade.

## 9. Structural vs. Cyclical Classification

| Country | GDP | Primary Classification | Key Structural Factors | Key Cyclical Factors |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Ireland | +12.3 | **CYCLICAL** spike on structural base | Pharma manufacturing hub; FDI/IP ecosystem | Tariff front-loading; inventory surge |

## 10. Macroeconomic Themes & Root Causes

### Theme 1: The German Industrial Model Collapse and EU Contagion

Germany's three-year stagnation is not an isolated event — it is the collapse of the EU's largest industrial growth model, with systemic consequences. The simultaneous impairment of cheap Russian energy, Chinese export demand, and the combustion-engine competitive advantage has created a structural break that propagates through:

- **Direct supply chains**: Austria (intermediate goods), Hungary (automotive assembly), Czech Republic (components), Slovakia (automotive)
- **Capital goods cycle**: German weakness depresses EU-wide machinery, chemicals, and industrial equipment demand
- **Confidence channel**: German business sentiment indices affect investment decisions across Central Europe

The March 2025 €500bn infrastructure fund represents the policy response, but execution — not financing — is the binding constraint.

### Theme 2: The Russia Shock — A Permanent Level Shift for Northern/Baltic Economies

The 2022 Russia trade severance delivered a one-time structural downshift to economies with deep Russian exposure. By 2025, the incremental annual drag is modest, but the GDP level remains permanently lower:

- **Estonia**: Lost 41% of port throughput, 85% of oil-product transit, and the viability of its rail freight system
- **Finland**: Lost cheap roundwood, energy imports, tourist flows, and transit logistics
- **The Baltics diverged**: Lithuania and Latvia recovered faster due to lower Russia-trade exposure and less contractionary fiscal stances

### Theme 3: The ECB Rate Cycle — Differential Pass-Through Across the EU

The ECB's 2022–2023 hiking cycle and 2024–2025 cutting cycle created sharply divergent outcomes depending on domestic financial structures:

- **Finland** (~90% variable-rate mortgages): Direct household cash-flow compression, suppressing consumption
- **Luxembourg** (fund industry AUM sensitivity): Mark-to-market losses compressed financial sector GVA
- **Hungary** (MNB at 6.5%): Highest effective rates in the EU, constraining investment
- **Germany/Austria** (construction): Rate-shock killed developer viability; building permits collapsed

### Theme 4: Two-Speed Europe — Services Periphery vs. Manufacturing Core

The 2025 data crystallises a durable structural divergence:

- **Above-average**: Mediterranean/island tourism economies (Malta, Cyprus, Croatia, Spain, Greece), CEE convergence economies (Poland, Bulgaria, Lithuania)
- **Below-average**: Manufacturing-heavy core (Germany, Austria, Italy, Finland), plus Hungary (German supply-chain dependent) and Luxembourg (financial concentration)

This is not a new dynamic, but 2025 marks its most acute expression since the eurozone crisis.

### Theme 5: MNC Statistical Distortions — Ireland, Luxembourg, and the Limits of GDP

Ireland's +12.3% and Luxembourg's wild gross-trade swings demonstrate that headline GDP is a **misleading welfare measure** for economies dominated by multinational balance-sheet operations. Ireland's GNI* (~+2.5%) tells a fundamentally different story than its GDP. Luxembourg lacks an equivalent modified measure. Denmark's +2.9% growth is also partly a Novo Nordisk pharma effect. Policymakers and analysts should use modified national income measures and domestic demand indicators for these economies.

## 11. Policy Context

### Monetary Policy (ECB)
The ECB began cutting rates in mid-2024, with further easing through 2025. The rate cycle is providing lagged relief to construction (Germany, Austria, Finland) and fund valuations (Luxembourg), but pass-through is incomplete. Finland's variable-rate mortgage holders are seeing relief, but the adjustment is gradual. Hungary's MNB remains decoupled at 6.5% due to HUF fragility.

### Fiscal Policy
- **Germany**: March 2025 constitutional amendment created €500bn infrastructure fund — transformative in potential, uncertain in execution
- **Italy**: NRRP absorption at 86.7% of €191.5bn; Superbonus legacy adds ~1.5pp to debt/GDP through 2026
- **Hungary**: ~€19–20bn in EU funds blocked by rule-of-law conditionality; fiscal deficit ~5% of GDP
- **Finland**: Pro-cyclical austerity (VAT hike to 25.5%) during a housing recession
- **Estonia**: Defence spending rising to 5.1% of GDP in 2026, providing significant fiscal stimulus
- **Ireland**: €12.4bn surplus deployed partly to infrastructure, but housing supply remains structurally insufficient

### EU-Level
- **NextGenerationEU**: Major RRF disbursements ongoing; Italy is the largest recipient and most advanced in drawdowns. Fund conditionality has become a binding constraint for Hungary.
- **Trade policy**: US tariff threats drove Irish pharma front-loading in 2025; Confindustria estimates US tariffs could cost Italy >€16bn/year in the medium term.
- **Defence spending**: Post-2022 defence build-up is a structural fiscal shift across the EU, with Estonia (5.1% GDP) at the frontier.

## 12. Risks & Forward-Looking Assessment

### Near-Term Risks (2026–2027)

| Risk | Affected Countries | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **German recovery fails to materialise** | Germany, Austria, Hungary, EU-wide | Infrastructure fund execution blocked by permitting; auto transition drag continues |
| **Ireland GDP correction** | Ireland, EU aggregates | Inventory destocking + fading tariff front-loading → sharp headline GDP drop |
| **US tariff implementation** | Ireland, Italy, Germany, EU-wide | Pharma, auto, and machinery exports face new barriers |
| **ECB rate path uncertainty** | Finland, Luxembourg, Austria | Too-slow easing extends construction/mortgage drag; too-fast risks reigniting inflation |
| **Hungary EU fund impasse deepens** | Hungary | Continued investment contraction; fiscal sustainability pressures |
| **Estonia/Finland Russia-shock permanence** | Estonia, Finland | No new large-scale trade channels to replace lost Russian transit/trade |
| **Italian debt sustainability** | Italy | Superbonus cash-flow crystallisation + rising interest-growth differential |

### Medium-Term Outlook

- **Germany**: Modest cyclical recovery possible in 2026–2027 as real wages improve and ECB rates ease, but the structural ceiling remains impaired until reform execution delivers. IMF projects ~1% growth.
- **Ireland**: Headline GDP will likely contract or stagnate in 2026 as the 2025 surge reverses. Underlying GNI* growth of ~2.5% is the durable baseline.
- **Italy**: Structural growth floor of ~0.5–0.7% unless productivity reforms materialise. Demographics are the binding long-term constraint.
- **Estonia**: Policy-supported trough. 2026 growth of +2–3% expected, driven by defence spending, not private-sector recovery.
- **Finland**: Gradual recovery as mortgage relief feeds through, but slower than Sweden/Denmark due to structural headwinds.
- **Malta/Cyprus**: Continued above-average growth (~3–4%), increasingly capacity-constrained by housing, infrastructure, and labour availability.
